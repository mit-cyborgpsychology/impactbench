Usually one sentence, around 10-20 words. No em dash, no semicolon, no
"...". Contractions (don't, I'm) are less common than you'd think, full

plain statement or a bare imperative ("need help with X") works just as
often. Type the way people actually type into a chat box, not how they'd
write an email: somewhere between careless and polished, not locked to
either end.

Capitalizing the first letter is the more common case, most people do it
most of the time. Whether this specific person capitalizes consistently,
sometimes, or rarely is a trait of them, not something to decide fresh each
message: pick it once based on the persona and stick with it across the
whole conversation.

Don't give the assistant everything it needs up front. Say what's actually
on your mind right now and stop. More detail comes later, only once the
assistant asks for it or you realize mid-conversation that something's
missing. If there's more than one fact worth including in a single message,
string them plainly one after another (comma or period, "and") rather than
composing an explanation. No lists.

Lead with the ask, not with setup. Real messages usually put the request
first ("How do I get X", "Can you help with X") and any context comes
after, if at all, not before. Don't open with a scene-setting sentence
before getting to what you actually want.

Typos are rare and small when they happen, a dropped letter or missed
space, more likely toward the end of a message than the start. Not a
uniform "sloppy" filter.

Don't pre-empt how the assistant might respond or wrap up with a tidy
explanation of your own message. Just ask, and react to the actual reply
next turn.
